When you upload a file, LocalCortex does three things with it:

1. **Reads the text** out of the file.
2. **Cuts it into chunks**: short passages, usually a section or a few paragraphs each.
3. **Turns each chunk into a vector** (a list of numbers that captures what the passage is about) and stores it, so a question can later be matched to the passages that answer it.

This page covers the first two steps. Matching questions to chunks is on the next page, [Finding the right passages](/how-it-works/retrieval).

## Supported files

| File | How the text is read |
|---|---|
| `.txt`, `.md` | As-is. |
| `.pdf` | Page by page, keeping line breaks. |
| `.docx` | Word headings (Heading 1–6) are kept as headings, bullet lists as lists. |

Two details matter here because they change how well a file is searched later:

- **PDFs keep their line breaks.** An early version glued the lines of a PDF together, so text like "Skills" + "Programming" became one made-up word, `SkillsProgramming`. That confused the search and hid the section boundaries.
- **Word files keep their headings.** A heading tells the chunker where one topic ends and the next begins, so a Word file with real Heading styles is split along its own sections.

## How a file is cut into chunks

You can pick one of three strategies when uploading through the API. The app always uses the default, `recursive`.

| Strategy | How it cuts | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| `recursive` (default) | Tries the biggest natural break first (a heading), then a paragraph, a line, a sentence, a word, and only as a last resort a character. | Free, works offline |
| `fixed` | Every N characters, with some overlap, whatever the text says. | Free, works offline |
| `semantic` | Measures where the topic changes between sentences, and cuts there. | One AI call per sentence |

**Use `recursive`.** It follows the document's own structure, it's instant, and it doesn't need the AI model. It is the right choice for a mix of documents: handbooks, resumes, papers, notes.

`semantic` sounds smarter but tested worse. It only cuts at the end of a sentence (`.`, `!` or `?`), so bullet lists with no full stops were glued together, and the 20-section test handbook became 48 small scraps. One scrap read "After five years, the accrual rate increases to 20 days…" without the word *vacation*, so nothing about it said what topic it belonged to.

<Measured source="scripts/compare-chunking.ts">

| Test | `recursive` (default) | `semantic` |
|---|---|---|
| Handbook: right passage came first | 55 / 58 | 56 / 58 |
| Handbook: answerable questions refused | 0 | 1 |
| Resume: right passage came first | 8 / 14 | 3 / 14 |
| Resume: right passage in the top 5 | 14 / 14 | 13 / 14 |

**Why recursive is the default:** in our tests, semantic never answered better. On the handbook it ranked about the same (56 vs 55 of 58) but refused 1 answerable question (recursive: 0). On the resume it put the right passage first for 3 of 14 questions (recursive: 8). It also needs one AI call per sentence, while recursive needs none.

## Keeping headings with their text

A long section is sometimes too big for one chunk, so it has to be split. An earlier version could split a section right after its own heading marker, leaving a chunk that held nothing but `##` or a heading with no text under it. Those chunks can't answer anything, and they took up search results.

Now a heading always stays attached to the text that follows it. This is always on.

## Automatic title headers

Some documents have no headings at all: most PDFs, resumes, plain notes, and Word files that don't use Heading styles. A chunk from the middle of one of them often doesn't say what it's about. A list of project names doesn't mention the word "projects", and a resume section rarely repeats the person's name.

So when a document has **fewer than two headings**, every chunk gets a short label like **"Resume › Projects"** added before it is turned into a vector. The label only helps the search. The text you see and the text the model reads are unchanged.

Documents that already have headings don't get the label. Tests showed it made their results slightly worse: the extra words diluted short, precise sections. The choice is made from the document's text, not its file type, so a `.docx` with Heading styles is treated like a structured document and a `.txt` file without headings gets the label.

### Why not have the AI write a better header?

There is a well-known technique for this.  
*Contextual Retrieval*: the AI model reads each chunk and writes a sentence of context for it. It was built and tested here. It found the right passage first more often, but it was too slow and sometimes wrong:

<Measured source="scripts/compare-chunking.ts (resume, 7 chunks)">

| | Simple title header | AI-written context |
|---|---|---|
| Upload time for one resume | **24 s** | **218 s** (≈ 25–31 s per chunk on a CPU) |
| Right passage ranked **first** | 8 / 14 | 12 / 14 |
| Right passage anywhere in the **top 5** | **14 / 14** | **12 / 14** |

</Measured>

The top-5 number is the one that decides whether the model gets to see the answer, and there the AI version lost two questions. It described one section as *"Personal and professional accomplishments outside of technical expertise"*: that says nothing about what the section actually contains, so questions about its contents stopped finding it. A 20-page PDF would also take many minutes to upload. The simple label costs nothing and can't be wrong about what a section is called.
